Aus Phone Towers — User Guide

Aus Phone Towers maps the mobile phone towers — and, when you ask for them, the radio, TV and other licensed transmitters — anywhere in Australia. This guide covers the iPhone app and the web edition at ausphonetowers.com.au, which are the same app. It downloads each site from the ACMA licence register as you move around the map, and draws the tower, the transmitters licensed there and an estimate of how far their signal reaches.

Where the data comes from: tower positions, frequencies, power and antenna details come from the ACMA licence and HRP (antenna radiation pattern) datasets. Coverage shapes are calculated estimates, not official carrier coverage maps — they are what the licensed transmitter power and antenna pattern predict, before buildings and trees.

1. Finding your way around

The map is the app. Everything else opens as a panel that slides up from the bottom and slides away again. Close any panel by swiping it down or by tapping the map above it.

Across the top of the screen:

A ⓘ Legend chip sits at the top-left of the map at all times. It explains the pin colours, the coverage shading and the ring labels without leaving the map.

Zoom in to load towers. Sites are downloaded for the area you are looking at, a tile at a time, as you pan. Zoomed out so far that a whole region is on screen, the app stops downloading and says Zoom in further to download towers… — zoom back in and they appear.

2. Tower pins

Every transmitter site is a teardrop pin, coloured by the licensee that operates it and marked with its initial. Pins are also tilted at a different angle per carrier, so when several carriers share one tower the pins fan out from the same point and each one can still be seen and tapped.

Your own position is the standard Google Maps blue dot, not one of the app's pins. It appears once you allow location access.

3. Coverage polygons

Tap a tower and the app draws its estimated coverage in the carrier's colour — the same colours as the pins, except that non-carrier transmitters are drawn in a deeper rose than their pale pin so the shape stays visible on the map. Each transmitter's shape is built from up to four nested contour rings — Maximum, Strong, Good and Weak, innermost to outermost — each marking how far that signal level is predicted to reach. Which rings are drawn depends on the Signal strength filter (section 5); the default is Good, so you get the Maximum, Strong and Good rings. While Follow GPS is on the app sets the filter aside and keeps all four rings visible. Their translucent fills stack, making the inner, stronger areas darker.

Two different things are encoded in a coverage shape, and it is worth keeping them apart:

  • Size is reach — how far the signal is predicted to travel. That depends on transmitter power, frequency, antenna pattern, the surrounding environment, and the transmitter's height above the terrain around it — a hilltop tower reaches further and a valley tower less. Low frequencies travel much further.
  • Depth of colour is capacity — a stronger tint means that transmitter can carry more data. Overlapping shapes also stack up and look deeper.

Each transmitter's outer ring carries a small label with its frequency and technology — 850 MHz 4G, 3510 MHz 5G and so on. The label sits on the ring's outer line at the point furthest from the tower, so on an irregular shape it can turn up at a different angle for every tower. That is expected.

  • Tap the same tower again to take its coverage off the map.
  • Tap a different tower and its coverage replaces the previous one — unless Multi-tower coverage is on (section 6), in which case each tower you tap is added to what is already drawn, which is how you compare neighbouring sites.
Not every transmitter has a published antenna pattern. A site without one gets a simple estimated shape from its licensed power, rather than nothing.

Polygon precision (Map Layers, section 6) controls how many points make up each ring: Low (faster, blockier), Medium (default) or High (smoother, more points, slower to draw). It only applies to coverage drawn after you change it — shapes already on the map keep their existing detail.

4. Follow GPS

Follow GPS is drive mode. While it is on, the map stays centred on you as you move, at whatever zoom you have chosen, and a ⦿ Driving · following GPS chip sits at the top of the map so the state is never in doubt. The toolbar icon is filled and tinted while it is on, and struck through while it is off.

What the map shows while you drive. Every tower whose coverage is on the map keeps all four nested signal contours from section 3, drawn a little stronger than usual so they stay readable at a glance. Coverage never changes the camera zoom — only your position moves the map.

Which way is up. On iPhone, Settings → Map rotation while driving decides how the map turns while Follow GPS is on:

SettingWhat it does
Travel direction (default)The map turns to face the way you are travelling, from your GPS course. It only updates while you are actually moving, so it does not spin about when you stop at the lights.
Phone orientationThe map turns to face the way you point the phone, from its compass. Useful when you are standing still and lining the map up with a tower you can see. If the phone reports no compass the app says so and leaves the map as it is.
OffThe app never turns the map for you.

Rotation is only ever applied by Follow GPS, so these settings do nothing while it is off. The web edition has no rotation settings.

Follow GPS is always started and stopped by you. Unlike the Android app, this edition never switches it on by itself when you start driving. It needs location access, keeps the GPS running and uses more battery, so turn it off when you arrive. It is not remembered between launches.

5. Filters

The funnel icon opens the filter panel, which controls what the map draws. The count at the top — “N sites shown” — counts the sites already downloaded as you have moved around the map, not every site in Australia. Your choices are remembered between launches.

GroupWhat it does
CarrierTelstra, Optus, Vodafone, NBN, Other.
Generation2G GSM, 3G UMTS, 4G LTE, 5G NR. One site often carries several.
FrequencyFive bands from below 700 MHz to 3.8 GHz and above. Low bands travel far and get inside buildings; high bands carry much more data but reach less far.
City densityMetropolitan, Urban, Suburban, Open — how built-up the area around the site is, which is what the coverage model assumes about obstructions.
Signal strengthMaximum, Strong, Good, Weak. Pick one — this is not a hide filter, it sets how far out the coverage rings are drawn. Default is Good. While Follow GPS is on, all four contours are shown (section 4).
Transmitter typeTelco, Radio, TV, Civil, Pager, CBRS, Aviation. Only Telco is on to begin with, so broadcast and specialist transmitters are hidden until you turn them on. Telco is a master switch for the Carrier chips.
Advanced ▾Tap to expand. Holds Multiplex — whether a 4G transmitter uses FD-LTE (separate send and receive frequencies, the common case) or TD-LTE (one frequency shared in time slots, common on fixed-wireless and small cells). NOT LTE covers every transmitter that is not 4G.
Two things that surprise people. The filter badge does not count chips that are off; it counts the groups with something switched off. Switch off 5G NR and the badge reads 1; switch off Optus as well and it reads 2. Radio, TV, civil, pager, CBRS and aviation never add to it, on or off — they start off, and that is the default. And the Telco chip and the Carrier chips move together: turning Telco off turns every carrier off, turning any carrier back on turns Telco back on, and turning the last carrier off turns Telco off.

7. Search

Search finds transmitter sites — not street addresses, not suburbs as places. Tap the magnifier, type part of a site name (site names usually contain the street and suburb), or a 4-digit postcode for a postcode match, and press Search (Enter on the web). Results are ranked nearest first from the middle of the map, and each row shows the site name, its state and postcode, and how far away it is. Very broad searches are capped at the first 50 matches. The back arrow closes the search box.

Tap a result and the map flies to that site and opens its details. If your device has Reduce Motion switched on, the map jumps there instead of flying. Your last ten searches are kept under Recent searches so you can repeat one with a tap; searching the same name again, in any letter case, moves it to the top rather than listing it twice, and Clear empties the list.

If the map moves but no site panel opens, the tower data for that area had not finished downloading. Tap the pin once it appears. If the map does not move at all, check that Lock map is off.

8. Tapping a tower — the site panel

Tapping any pin opens that site's panel from the bottom of the screen. It shows the site name, a chip naming the carrier in a wash of its colour, then the state, postcode and ACMA site ID. Below that are the site's latitude and longitude and, where the register has them, its ground elevation, the tower height and the city density class the coverage model uses for it (Metropolitan through to Open).

Then comes the transmitter table, one row per band:

ColumnMeaning
Gen2G, 3G, 4G, 5G — or LP for narrowband IoT.
FrequencyThe band's centre frequency.
BandwidthHow much spectrum that transmitter uses. More bandwidth, more capacity.
MIMOHow many antenna paths serve that band — 4x, 8x and so on. More paths means more simultaneous data streams.
LTEFD or TD duplexing, blank for non-4G.
CapacityA theoretical peak for that band, shared by everyone connected to that sector. It is not the speed you would get.
Several antennas on the same frequency roll up into one row, and the MIMO column shows how many there are. Broadcast sites (radio, TV and so on) get a different table: frequency, emission designator, call sign and capacity. If the panel says Device data still downloading…, close it and tap the pin again in a moment.

Two buttons sit at the bottom of the panel:

ButtonWhat it does
DirectionsHands the site's coordinates to Google Maps.
ACMA ↗Opens this site's official record in the ACMA Register of Radiocommunications Licences, in your browser.

9. Exporting map data

⋮ → Export Data offers three exports:

OptionContents
Export Towers (GeoJSON)
Export Towers (CSV)		Every tower gathered as you panned around — not just what is on screen right now — one entry per band, with its carrier, generation, frequency, bandwidth, power, azimuth and height. Either option writes both files.
Export Coverage (GeoJSON)One polygon per coverage ring currently drawn, with its tower and transmitter details, written as GeoJSON, CSV and KML for tools such as QGIS, CalTopo, ArcGIS Pro and Google Earth. Tap a tower to draw its coverage first.

The files are time-stamped and saved in the app's own documents folder; the message that follows lists each file's name. If there is nothing on the map to export, the app says so instead.

Known limits. On iPhone the exported files stay inside the app's private storage and cannot yet be shared out to Files or another app, and the web edition cannot export at all. The Android app can do both today; bringing the same to this edition is planned.
Why this exists: police search-and-rescue units export the tower position and antenna height into CalTopo or ArcGIS Pro and run a viewshed — a line-of-sight analysis that produces a realistic search area, often around half the area claimed by carrier coverage maps.

11. Supporting the app

This is a hobby project with real running costs. ⋮ → Support the App is the one place in the app where anything is sold, and every price shown there comes live from the App Store:

  • Donations — Morning Coffee, Coffee and Cake and Thanks For Lunch. They can be repeated and they do not remove ads.
  • One Year Ad Free — removes the banner for twelve months, after which ads return and it can be bought again.
  • Permanent Ad Free — removes the banner for good.
  • Rate the app — costs nothing and helps a great deal.

While you are ad-free the two ad-free options are hidden, so the app cannot sell you the same thing twice. Purchases are restored automatically on each launch; if ads ever come back when they should not, use Settings → Restore Purchases. About once a week the same page appears by itself; Maybe later dismisses it. The web edition has no ads, and purchases can only be made in the iPhone app.

12. Things worth knowing

  • Location permission is optional. Without it you still get the whole map; you just do not see your blue dot, the map does not start where you are, and Follow GPS and Phone orientation are unavailable.
  • The map reopens where you left it when you come back to the app, unless Follow GPS is on, in which case your live position wins.
  • Coverage shapes are predictions from licence data, not measurements. Real coverage depends on terrain, buildings and your device.
  • Dark mode follows your system setting and styles the whole app — the toolbar, the panels, this guide and the messages that pop up — but Google only allows the Normal basemap to be darkened. In Terrain, Satellite or Hybrid the map itself stays light.
  • Ads appear as a banner at the bottom of the map on iPhone. Support the App offers the ad-free purchases (section 11).
  • Refresh Data (⋮ menu) clears everything the app has downloaded this session and loads the current view again. Use it if the map looks stale or a download was interrupted.

13. What the Android app has that this edition does not

The Android app can read the mobile modem; iPhones and web browsers do not let an app see which cell it is connected to. Everything that depends on that is therefore Android-only. The rest — the tower map, coverage polygons, filters, search, legend, map layers, multi-tower coverage, lock map and polygon precision — works the same in both.
